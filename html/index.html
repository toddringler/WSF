Why I Started Wild Steelhead Futures

I started Wild Steelhead Futures (WSF) because I am concerned that changing ocean conditions may threaten the long-term survival of wild steelhead across their entire range. I care deeply about protecting wild places and the creatures whose lives depend on them. Wild steelhead connect mountainous headwater streams to the vast expanse of the Pacific Ocean. Their survival depends on conditions across that extraordinary range, yet responsibility for their conservation is divided among institutions, jurisdictions, and areas of expertise.

I bring decades of experience in environmental science, conservation, and public policy to this concern. Trained as a climate scientist, I conducted research on ocean dynamics and climate change, led a twenty-person climate modeling team at Los Alamos National Laboratory, helped coordinate climate science at the Department of Energy, and contributed to numerous international scientific programs.

My commitment to protecting wild places, informed by my understanding of a changing climate, led me into environmental policy and politics in the western United States. I served on the board of WildEarth Guardians and as a science advisor to New Mexico Citizens’ Climate Lobby. In Congress, I advised a New Mexico U.S. senator on environmental policy and managed a legislative portfolio addressing western water settlements, drought, wildfire resilience, and energy and climate policy. At the Science and Technology Policy Institute, I provided policy analysis for the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy.

Working across science, conservation, and government has taught me that responding to environmental threats requires both understanding the science and understanding how institutions set priorities, divide responsibilities, and turn knowledge into action. Through WSF, I want to examine whether conservation actions align with the biological threats facing wild steelhead across their range—and what more may be needed for wild steelhead to thrive over the long term.

I am beginning with two projects: a census of NGO actions intended to protect wild steelhead, and a discussion of how well conservation efforts are addressing ocean-related risks to steelhead survival. I will seek feedback from the conservation community on the usefulness of both projects. That work will help me assess whether WSF can make a useful contribution, whether an ongoing effort is warranted, and what form it might take.

Foundational Hypothesis

The wild steelhead conservation activity spans numerous organizations, jurisdictions, life stages, and environments. This federated approach has created substantial expertise and capability, but may also make it difficult to develop a holistic understanding of the challenges facing wild steelhead across their range. It may also create unintended or unmanaged gaps—risks, issues, or conservation needs that fall between organizational mandates, geographic boundaries, or areas of responsibility, and for which no individual institution assumes clear ownership.

The wild steelhead conservation community may benefit from an independent initiative that examines the broader picture, integrates knowledge across disciplines and institutions, identifies potential gaps and systemic blind spots, and explores strategic questions that extend beyond individual organizations, jurisdictions, or conservation programs.

Wild Steelhead Futures exists to investigate this possibility, evaluate where an integrated perspective might add value, and freely share its research, analysis, and strategic synthesis.

Wild Steelhead Futures: An Initial Approach

On this page

  • Seek Feedback and Observe Use
  • Assess the Hypothesis and Decide What Comes Next
  • How We Will Carry Out the Initial Work

The foundational hypothesis asks whether an independent, range-wide perspective could make a useful contribution to wild steelhead conservation. We will test that possibility through two initial projects, community feedback, and an assessment of whether further effort is warranted.

Two Initial Projects

Both projects will begin by examining relevant existing work. Their value will depend on whether they contribute useful information, connections, or perspectives beyond what is already available.

Two projects provide concrete starting points for testing the foundational hypothesis. One would bring together information about the conservation community and its work; the other would examine the relationship between biological risks and conservation actions.

A Web-Based Database of Organizations and Conservation Actions

Proposed project: A freely accessible, web-based database of organizations involved in wild steelhead conservation and the actions they undertake. The aim would be to build toward a comprehensive resource spanning the wild steelhead range, with information that people can explore by geography, life stage, biological threat, and type of action.

An initial version would establish the structure and populate a manageable portion of the database, making its coverage and limitations clear. It would give the community something to use and assess: Does bringing this information together make it easier to understand who is doing what, find relevant expertise and work, and see connections or potential gaps across conservation efforts?

A Discussion of Marine-Phase Biological Risks and Conservation Actions

Proposed project: A discussion of the biological risks wild steelhead face during the marine phase and how existing conservation actions address those risks. The work would bring together available knowledge about marine survival, identify relevant conservation efforts, and examine their alignment with the risks described, including available evidence of their effectiveness.

The purpose would be to identify where actions appear responsive to known risks, where important risks may receive limited attention, and where uncertainty prevents a clear assessment. It would also consider what can realistically be addressed through conservation action. This would provide a concrete example of whether examining threats and actions across institutional, jurisdictional, and geographic boundaries yields useful insights.

Both projects should offer something useful in their own right.

Seek Feedback and Observe Use

Both projects will be freely available for the community to access, use, adapt, and share, with software released under an open-source license and original written content and database materials shared under appropriate Creative Commons licenses.

With this work in hand, WSF will invite people across the wild steelhead conservation community to examine, use, and critique it. These conversations will explore both the usefulness of the projects and the broader need for the kind of contribution they represent.

The conversations will address three questions:

  • Is the work useful? Does it clarify conservation challenges, inform decisions, or help people identify opportunities for action? Where is it incomplete, duplicative, or unconvincing?
  • What else is needed? What important questions, connections, or conservation needs are receiving insufficient attention?
  • Is there a useful role for WSF? Would further independent research, analysis, and synthesis help, and how could it complement existing efforts?

WSF will document feedback and examples of use, including differing views and criticism. This will provide a basis for assessing the hypothesis and deciding what, if anything, should follow.

Assess the Hypothesis and Decide What Comes Next

WSF will bring together the findings from the initial projects, community feedback, and examples of use to assess the foundational hypothesis. The central question is whether an independent effort examining conservation actions and outcomes across organizational, geographic, and biological boundaries offers sufficient value to warrant further work.

This assessment will consider what the projects revealed, whether people found the work useful, and what needs emerged through discussion. It will also distinguish between limitations of the particular projects and limitations of the underlying idea. A useful project may not establish the need for an ongoing initiative; limited uptake may reflect shortcomings in the work or how it was shared.

The assessment will address three questions:

  • What have we learned about the hypothesis? Where does the evidence support it, challenge it, or suggest that it should be revised? What remains uncertain?
  • Is further effort warranted? Is there a clear need that WSF could help address, and how would that contribution complement existing work?
  • What should happen next? Should WSF develop an ongoing program, undertake another limited test, pursue the work through collaboration with an existing organization, or conclude the initiative?

WSF will document and freely share this assessment and the reasoning behind its next steps. If an ongoing effort is warranted, the findings will provide the foundation for a strategy defining its purpose, priorities, scope, and resource needs.

How We Will Carry Out the Initial Work

The initial work will follow a few practical principles:

  • Keep the scope manageable. Match project commitments to available time, expertise, and resources, with clear responsibilities and completion points.
  • Make the work credible. Use appropriate sources, methods, and review; distinguish evidence from interpretation and make uncertainty explicit.
  • Maintain independence and transparency. Disclose funding, affiliations, and potential conflicts of interest.
  • Make the work open and usable. Share outputs, methods, and supporting materials in accessible forms, consistent with the licensing commitments above.
  • Respect contributors and their knowledge. Respect the rights, responsibilities, and knowledge of Indigenous peoples and other contributors, including any limits on sharing information.

Before beginning each project, WSF will define its scope, intended output, resource needs, and review process. These arrangements will remain proportionate to the exploratory nature of the work.

A Census of NGO Actions to Protect Wild Steelhead

Draft project description — proposed initial project.

Purpose

Create a freely accessible, web-based census of actions by nongovernmental organizations intended to protect wild steelhead. The census will help people understand who is doing what across the wild steelhead range, find relevant work and expertise, and explore connections or potential gaps in conservation efforts.

Nongovernmental organizations often bridge scientific knowledge and conservation action and are at the forefront of responding to emerging needs. A census of their activities can help reveal how conservation priorities are being translated into action.

Initial Scope

The census will take a range-wide perspective, beginning with a manageable set of organizations and actions. Its geographic coverage, sources, and limitations will be clearly stated.

Actions could include research, monitoring, habitat restoration, fisheries policy advocacy, and public engagement. Inclusion will not imply endorsement or demonstrated effectiveness.

The initial census will focus on actions currently underway. Each entry will record its status and when its sources were last reviewed. Completed actions may be included where they provide essential context, but will be clearly distinguished from ongoing work.

What Counts as an Action

An action is a distinct project, program, or policy effort that an organization undertakes to protect wild steelhead. Each entry will describe what is being done, where, and which conservation need it addresses. An organization’s general mission or statement of concern will not, by itself, count as an action.

Approach

The census will be built from publicly available documents, including organizational websites, project descriptions, reports, and other published materials. Each entry will cite its sources. Where those materials do not establish an action’s status or scope, the uncertainty will be made explicit.

  • Examine existing directories, databases, and related work before defining what the census will add.
  • Establish a consistent structure for recording organizations, actions, geography, life stages, and biological threats addressed.
  • Document sources and the date information was reviewed; distinguish reported information from WSF interpretation.
  • Populate an initial version and invite organizations and users to identify errors, omissions, and useful improvements.

Initial Outputs

A working web-based database, accompanied by a description of its methods, coverage, and limitations.

A companion summary will describe the initial coverage, patterns in documented conservation actions, and questions that warrant further examination. It will distinguish gaps in the census from potential gaps in conservation activity; an action missing from public documentation cannot be assumed not to exist.

Software will be released under an open-source license, and original documentation and database materials under appropriate Creative Commons licenses, respecting restrictions on third-party material.

What We Will Test

Does bringing this information together help people find relevant expertise, understand the conservation landscape, or identify needs that merit closer examination? Feedback and examples of use will help determine whether the resource warrants further development.

Next Steps

The next step is to develop a detailed project plan defining the initial coverage, methods, database structure, and software requirements. That plan will guide development of a first version for community use and feedback and will be made available here.

Marine-Phase Biological Risks and Conservation Actions

Draft project description — proposed initial discussion paper.

Purpose

Examine how well conservation efforts are addressing ocean-related risks to wild steelhead survival.

Concern about changing ocean conditions motivates this focus on the marine phase. By considering biological risks and conservation actions together, the paper will identify conservation needs that may be difficult to see from the perspective of any single organization or jurisdiction.

Initial Scope

The discussion will take a range-wide perspective while making geographic differences and limits in the evidence explicit. It will examine available knowledge rather than presume that risks, causes of mortality, or appropriate responses are the same for every population.

The discussion will consider relevant actions by NGOs, government agencies, and other institutions. The NGO census will provide a starting point, while additional sources will help establish the broader conservation response.

The specific risks, geographic examples, and period covered will be defined through an initial review of existing research and conservation work.

Approach

The discussion will distinguish actions intended to understand or monitor a risk from those intended to reduce it, while recognizing that both may be necessary.

  • Review relevant research and existing syntheses concerning marine survival and biological risks.
  • Draw on the NGO census and other relevant sources to identify conservation actions addressing those risks, including available evidence of their effectiveness.
  • Compare risks and responses, distinguishing apparent gaps in action from gaps in knowledge, authority, or practical capacity.
  • Seek substantive review and clearly separate established findings, interpretation, and unresolved questions.

Initial Output

A discussion paper bringing together:

  • A synthesis of documented marine-phase risks to wild steelhead survival.
  • An overview of relevant conservation actions, drawing on the NGO census and other sources.
  • An assessment of how those actions align with the risks, including evidence of effectiveness and limits to current knowledge.
  • Questions and potential opportunities for further research, collaboration, or conservation action.

Original written content will be freely available under an appropriate Creative Commons license, with sources attributed and restrictions on third-party material respected.

What We Will Test

Does examining marine-phase risks alongside conservation actions yield useful insights for the conservation community? Feedback will help assess the analysis, identify missing perspectives, and determine whether further independent synthesis would add value.

Next Steps

The next step is to develop a detailed project plan defining the paper’s initial scope, guiding questions, research approach, and review process. The plan will identify the resources needed and define when an initial paper will be ready for community feedback. It will be made available here.
