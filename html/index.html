An integrated perspective on the future of wild steelhead.

Wild Steelhead Futures asks a narrow question: what do wild steelhead need to persist over the long term? It examines how conservation efforts align with the biological threats facing wild steelhead across their entire range, from mountain headwaters to the far reaches of the North Pacific.

Through initial projects and community feedback, I am testing whether an independent, range-wide perspective across institutions, jurisdictions, and environments can help identify gaps and inform conservation decisions.

Why I Started Wild Steelhead Futures

I started Wild Steelhead Futures (WSF) because I am concerned that changing ocean conditions may threaten the long-term survival of wild steelhead across their entire range. Few species tie together as many places, and as many risks, as wild steelhead. Their survival depends on an unbroken chain of suitable habitat, from mountain headwaters to the far reaches of the North Pacific and back, yet responsibility for their conservation is divided among Tribes and First Nations, governments, conservation organizations, and many areas of expertise.

I bring decades of experience in environmental science, conservation, and public policy to this concern. Trained as a climate scientist, I conducted research on ocean dynamics and climate change, led a twenty-person climate modeling team at Los Alamos National Laboratory, helped coordinate climate science at the Department of Energy, and contributed to numerous international scientific programs. I am not a fisheries biologist. I come to steelhead through the ocean, where my career has been focused and where I suspect the risks are hardest for any single organization to address. That perspective is a prior, a starting point that I will update as the evidence comes in. Nothing would make me happier than to learn that marine risks are not rising beyond their already harsh reality.

My commitment to protecting wild places, informed by my understanding of a changing climate, led me into environmental policy in the western United States. I have served on the board and advisory groups of conservation organizations, worked in the U.S. Senate on western water settlements, drought, wildfire resilience, and energy and climate policy, and provided policy analysis for the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy.

Working across science, conservation, and government has taught me that responding to environmental threats requires both understanding the science and understanding how institutions set priorities, divide responsibilities, and turn knowledge into action. Through WSF, I want to examine whether conservation actions align with the biological threats facing wild steelhead across their range—and what more may be needed for them to thrive over the long term. If it turns out that conservation efforts are already well matched to those threats, that is worth knowing too.

I am beginning with two projects: a census of NGO actions intended to protect wild steelhead, and a discussion of how well conservation efforts are addressing ocean-related risks to steelhead survival. I will seek feedback from the conservation community on the usefulness of both projects. That work will help me assess whether WSF can make a useful contribution, whether an ongoing effort is warranted, and what form it might take.

My aim is to be useful to everyone working on behalf of wild steelhead. Finding a gap is not a criticism of anyone. It is an opportunity to do more for the fish, together.

Working Premise

Wild steelhead conservation is carried out by numerous organizations, working across jurisdictions, life stages, and environments. This federated approach has built substantial expertise and capability. It can also make it harder to develop a holistic understanding of the challenges facing wild steelhead across their range, and it can leave gaps: risks, issues, or conservation needs that fall between organizational mandates, geographic boundaries, or areas of responsibility, and for which no single institution has clear ownership.

Gaps can also arise within mandates. Each institution balances wild steelhead against other legitimate responsibilities, such as fishing opportunity, hatchery production, hydropower, and water supply. Those trade-offs are real, and someone has to make them. WSF carries none of those responsibilities, which gives it the freedom to ask a narrow question: what do wild steelhead need to persist over the long term? WSF has its own vantage point too, and its work should face the same scrutiny as anyone's.

I believe the wild steelhead conservation community would benefit from an independent initiative that brings a broad view to that narrow question, complementing existing work by integrating knowledge across disciplines and institutions, identifying gaps and systemic blind spots, and exploring strategic questions that extend beyond any one organization, jurisdiction, or conservation program.

Wild Steelhead Futures exists to test this premise: to ask what wild steelhead need across their whole range and life cycle, how well actions across the board meet those needs, and where an integrated perspective adds value. It will share its research, analysis, and strategic synthesis freely. If the evidence shows that existing organizations already cover the major risks, that is a useful finding too.

Wild Steelhead Futures: An Initial Approach

On this page

  • Seek Feedback and Learn How the Work Is Used
  • Assess the Premise and Decide What Comes Next
  • How I Will Carry Out the Initial Work

The working premise asks whether an independent, range-wide perspective could make a useful contribution to wild steelhead conservation. I will test that possibility through two initial projects, community feedback, and an assessment of whether further effort is warranted. I expect this initial phase, from the first projects through the assessment, to take about 12 months, concluding in late 2027.

Two Initial Projects

Two projects provide concrete starting points for testing the working premise. One will bring together information about NGO conservation actions; the other will examine the relationship between biological risks and conservation actions.

Both projects will begin by examining relevant existing work. Their value will depend on whether they contribute useful information, connections, or perspectives beyond what is already available.

A Census of NGO Actions to Protect Wild Steelhead

Proposed project: A freely accessible, web-based census of actions by nongovernmental organizations intended to protect wild steelhead. The aim is to build toward a comprehensive resource spanning the wild steelhead range, with information that people can explore by geography, life stage, biological threat, and type of action.

An initial version will establish the structure and populate a manageable portion of the census, making its coverage and limitations clear. It will give the community something to use and assess: Does bringing this information together make it easier to understand who is doing what, find relevant expertise, and see connections or gaps?

A Discussion of Marine-Phase Biological Risks and Conservation Actions

Proposed project: A discussion of the biological risks wild steelhead face during the marine phase and how existing conservation actions address those risks. The work will bring together available knowledge about marine survival, identify relevant conservation efforts, and examine their alignment with the risks described, including what is already known about their effectiveness.

The purpose is to identify where actions appear responsive to known risks, where important risks may receive limited attention, and where uncertainty prevents a clear assessment. It will also consider what can realistically be addressed through conservation action. Finally, it will test, with a concrete example, whether looking across organizational and geographic boundaries reveals anything new.

Seek Feedback and Learn How the Work Is Used

With this work in hand, I will invite people across the wild steelhead conservation community to examine, use, and critique it. Anyone can comment on any page of this site. I will also reach out directly to NGOs, agencies, Tribes and First Nations, and researchers.

The conversations will address three questions:

  • Is the work useful? Does it clarify conservation challenges, inform decisions, or help people identify opportunities for action? Where is it incomplete, duplicative, or unconvincing?
  • What else is needed? What important questions, connections, or conservation needs are receiving insufficient attention?
  • Is there a useful role for WSF? Would further independent research, analysis, and synthesis help, and how could it complement existing efforts?

I will document feedback and examples of use, including differing views and criticism. This will provide a basis for assessing the premise and deciding what, if anything, should follow.

Assess the Premise and Decide What Comes Next

I will bring together the findings from the initial projects, community feedback, and examples of use to assess the working premise. The central question is simple: is this kind of independent, range-wide work valuable enough to continue?

This assessment will consider what the projects revealed, whether people found the work useful, and what needs emerged through discussion. It will also distinguish between limitations of the particular projects and limitations of the underlying idea. A useful project may not establish the need for an ongoing initiative; limited uptake may reflect shortcomings in the work or how it was shared.

The assessment will address three questions:

  • What have I learned about the premise? Where does the evidence support it, challenge it, or suggest that it should be revised? What remains uncertain?
  • Is further effort warranted? Is there a clear need that WSF could help address, and how would that contribution complement existing work?
  • What should happen next? Should WSF develop an ongoing program, undertake another limited test, pursue the work through collaboration with an existing organization, or conclude the initiative?

I will document and freely share this assessment and the reasoning behind the next steps. If an ongoing effort is warranted, the findings will provide the foundation for a strategy defining its purpose, priorities, scope, and resource needs.

How I Will Carry Out the Initial Work

The initial work will follow a few practical principles:

  • Keep the scope manageable. Match project commitments to available time, expertise, and resources, with clear responsibilities and completion points.
  • Make the work credible. Use appropriate sources, methods, and review, including review by fisheries biologists and others with relevant expertise; distinguish evidence from interpretation and make uncertainty explicit.
  • Maintain independence and transparency. Disclose funding, affiliations, and potential conflicts of interest.
  • Make the work open and usable. Share outputs, methods, and supporting materials in accessible forms. Software will be released under an open-source license, and original written content and database materials under appropriate Creative Commons licenses, so the community can freely access, use, adapt, and share the work.
  • Respect contributors and their knowledge. Honor the rights, responsibilities, and knowledge of Tribes and First Nations and other contributors, including any limits on sharing information.

Before beginning each project, I will define its scope, intended output, resource needs, and review process. I will keep these plans as simple as the work allows.

A Census of NGO Actions to Protect Wild Steelhead

Draft project description.

Purpose

Create a freely accessible, web-based census of actions by nongovernmental organizations intended to protect wild steelhead: a documented, range-wide answer to the question of what the NGO community is doing, where, and against which threats. The census will provide the baseline for examining how conservation actions align with the biological risks wild steelhead face, and a practical resource for finding relevant work and expertise.

Motivation

A first step toward credibly identifying gaps between the biological risks facing wild steelhead and conservation actions is answering a basic question: what is the full scope of NGO work to protect wild steelhead? Without a reasonably complete answer, a claimed gap is only an impression. A thorough census also protects any one organization from being judged on an incomplete picture of the entire community's effort.

Answering that question is harder than it sounds. Wild steelhead conservation is carried out largely watershed by watershed, by many organizations working on different parts of the range and the life cycle. Each organization knows its own work well, but it can be difficult to see the whole: which threats are receiving attention, where, and from whom.

I am starting with nongovernmental organizations because they often connect scientific knowledge to conservation action and can respond quickly to emerging needs. Their work offers a useful window into how conservation priorities are being translated into action on the ground.

Initial Scope

The census will take a range-wide perspective, beginning with a manageable set of organizations and actions. Its geographic coverage, sources, and limitations will be clearly stated.

Actions could include research, monitoring, habitat restoration, policy advocacy, litigation, and public engagement. The census records what organizations are doing with the intent of protecting wild steelhead. It does not presume that any particular action helps. Whether a given action benefits wild steelhead is a question the census can help others examine, not one it answers. Inclusion does not imply endorsement or demonstrated effectiveness.

The initial census will focus on actions currently underway. Each entry will record its status and when its sources were last reviewed. Completed actions may be included where they provide essential context, but will be clearly distinguished from ongoing work.

Which Organizations Are Included

The census uses a simple test. An organization or activity is included if it is a U.S. 501(c)(3) nonprofit or a Canadian nonprofit organization, including registered charities; if it is made up of such organizations (such as a coalition); or if it operates under one (such as a fiscally sponsored project). Organizations that do not meet this test are outside the scope of this census.

Tribes and First Nations are sovereign governments. Their conservation actions, like those of federal, state, provincial, and local governments, fall outside this census and would be considered alongside other governments in any future work. Native-led nonprofits that meet the test above are included.

Organizations will be identified starting from known groups and expanding through the organizations they partner with, fund, or join in coalitions. Each decision to include or exclude an organization will be documented.

What Counts as an Action

An action is a distinct project, program, or policy effort that an organization undertakes to protect wild steelhead. Each entry will describe what is being done, where, and which conservation need it addresses. An organization’s general mission or statement of concern will not, by itself, count as an action.

Approach

The census will be built from publicly available documents, including organizational websites, project descriptions, reports, and other published materials. Each entry will cite its sources. Where those materials do not establish an action’s status or scope, the uncertainty will be made explicit.

  • Examine existing directories, databases, and related work before defining what the census will add.
  • Establish a consistent structure for recording organizations, actions, geography, life stages, and biological threats addressed.
  • Document sources and the date information was reviewed; distinguish reported information from WSF interpretation.
  • Populate an initial version. Invite every organization in the census to review its own entries, and invite all users to identify errors, omissions, and useful improvements. Corrections must point to publicly available documents, so that anyone can verify the census.

Initial Outputs

A working version of the census as a searchable website, accompanied by a description of its methods, coverage, and limitations.

A companion summary will describe the initial coverage, patterns in documented conservation actions, and questions that warrant further examination. It will distinguish gaps in the census from potential gaps in conservation activity; an action missing from public documentation cannot be assumed not to exist.

Software will be released under an open-source license, and original documentation and database materials under appropriate Creative Commons licenses, respecting restrictions on third-party material.

What I Will Test

Does bringing this information together help people find relevant expertise, understand the conservation landscape, or identify needs that merit closer examination? Feedback and examples of use will help determine whether the resource warrants further development.

Next Steps

The next step is to develop a detailed project plan defining the initial coverage, methods, database structure, and software requirements. That plan will guide development of a first version for community use and feedback and will be made available here. I expect the first version to be available in spring 2027.

Marine-Phase Biological Risks and Conservation Actions

Draft project description.

Purpose

Examine how well conservation efforts are addressing ocean-related risks to wild steelhead survival. By considering biological risks and conservation actions together, the paper aims to bring into view conservation needs that may be difficult to see from the perspective of any single organization or jurisdiction.

Motivation

Concern about changing ocean conditions motivates this focus on the marine phase, a concern this paper is meant to test, not assume.

A common view in steelhead conservation is that little can be done about the ocean, so effort is best focused on fresh water, where actions can make a direct difference. That focus may be right. What is often unclear is how it was reached: through a structured assessment of risks across the full life cycle, or by concentrating on the parts of the life cycle within each organization's reach. The distinction matters, because whether a risk is manageable cannot be known until it has been identified and assessed.

One reason for the freshwater focus may be structural. Much conservation is organized around individual streams and watersheds, and success is often measured by the number of fish returning to them. Every one of those populations shares the same ocean, but no watershed group is responsible for it. The ocean becomes everyone's concern and no one's responsibility, and a poor ocean year can become an explanation for low returns rather than a subject of study.

Risks that cannot be changed directly can still shape decisions everywhere else. Survival at sea depends in part on the size, timing, and condition of fish when they leave fresh water, so some marine-phase risk may be influenced through freshwater actions. And where the ocean cannot be managed, populations can be made better able to withstand it, by protecting the life-history and genetic diversity that keep a poor ocean year from affecting every population the same way.

Underlying all of this is a basic gap in knowledge: the causes of changing marine survival are not well understood. Ocean temperatures, competition with abundant pink salmon, predation, and changes in prey have all been proposed. Until the picture is clearer, it is difficult to judge which risks matter most, which can be addressed, and where conservation resources would do the most good.

This paper will not resolve these questions. Its aim is narrower: to bring together what is already known about marine-phase risks and the conservation response, set out clearly what remains uncertain, and help the conservation community decide which questions deserve more attention.

Initial Scope

The discussion will take a range-wide perspective while making geographic differences and limits in the evidence explicit. It will examine available knowledge rather than presume that risks, causes of mortality, or appropriate responses are the same for every population.

The discussion will consider relevant actions by NGOs, government agencies, and other institutions. The NGO census will provide a starting point, though many marine-relevant actions are likely carried out by governments, so additional sources will be essential to establish the broader conservation response.

The specific risks, geographic examples, and period covered will be defined through an initial review of existing research and conservation work.

Approach

The discussion will distinguish actions intended to understand or monitor a risk from those intended to reduce it, while recognizing that both may be necessary.

  • Review relevant research and existing syntheses concerning marine survival and biological risks.
  • Examine how marine survival has changed over time and weigh the evidence for competing explanations, without presuming a single cause.
  • Consider how conditions in fresh water and the diversity of populations affect survival at sea and resilience to poor ocean conditions.
  • Draw on the NGO census and other relevant sources to identify conservation actions addressing those risks, including what is already known about their effectiveness.
  • Compare risks and responses, distinguishing apparent gaps in action from gaps in knowledge, authority, or practical capacity.
  • Seek substantive review, including review by fisheries biologists and others with relevant expertise, and clearly separate established findings, interpretation, and unresolved questions.

Initial Output

A discussion paper bringing together:

  • A synthesis of documented marine-phase risks to wild steelhead survival, how they have changed over time, and the evidence for competing explanations.
  • An overview of relevant conservation actions, drawing on the NGO census and other sources.
  • An assessment of how those actions align with the risks, including what is already known about their effectiveness and the limits of current knowledge.
  • Questions and potential opportunities for further research, collaboration, or conservation action.

Original written content will be freely available under an appropriate Creative Commons license, with sources attributed and restrictions on third-party material respected.

What I Will Test

Does examining marine-phase risks alongside conservation actions yield useful insights for the conservation community? Feedback will help assess the analysis, identify missing perspectives, and determine whether further independent synthesis would add value.

Next Steps

The next step is to develop a detailed project plan defining the paper’s initial scope, guiding questions, research approach, and review process. The plan will identify the resources needed and confirm the schedule. It will be made available here. I expect a draft paper to be ready for community feedback in summer 2027.
